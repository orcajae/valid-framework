How a gate calibration works (T2, T3)

  1. Send the pipeline. Your code, your checks and their cutoffs. If your data licence bars sharing, our scripts run on your side.
  2. Rules fixed in advance. Test data with known answers and the pass rules are written down; you get their fingerprint before anything runs.
  3. Get the measurement. How often each check passes when nothing is there, how often it catches a planted edge, and which checks to keep, recalibrate or report differently. We do not tune the strategy.

Three levels

Fees are agreed in writing before any result exists. An allocator can commission T1 or T2 directly.

T1

Backtest Review

One backtest rerun from your code: leakage, costs, trading frequency, multiple testing, and the twelve VALID items (a proposed checklist, not yet tested as a set).

You receive written findings.

T2

Gate Calibration

The false-pass rate and power of your pass-or-fail checks, on data with known answers (each planted form named, since power depends on it), under rules fixed before the run.

You receive a report with rates, intervals and recommendations on the checks.

T3

Full Re-run

Your full pipeline rebuilt from code, with cost sensitivity, plus everything in T2.

You receive the T2 report plus pipeline findings.

A re-check after you change the pipeline is available at each level.

How an engagement works

  1. You write. A line on your pipeline and the checks it relies on. You get a reply within 2 business days (KST).
  2. Written scope. The level, the inputs, the deliverables, the time frame and the fee, agreed in writing before any work. If you want to share code before that, we can sign a mutual non-disclosure agreement.
  3. You send the materials. Code, the checks with their cutoffs, your confirmation that the data licence permits the analysis, and either the data or a request to run our scripts on your side. If your stated results cannot be reproduced from your code and data, the review stops and says so.
  4. Rules fixed (T2, T3). The test data with known answers and the pass rules are written down; you receive their fingerprint before anything runs.
  5. Draft report. You point out errors of fact and we correct them. You can correct facts, not conclusions.
  6. Final report. T1: written findings. T2: the report with rates, intervals and recommendations on the checks, plus the rules, scripts and outputs needed to reproduce it. T3: the same, plus pipeline findings.

Expected time from receiving complete code and data (or, when our scripts run on your side, the full output) to the final report, not counting the time you take to review the draft: T1 1–2 weeks, T2 3–4 weeks, T3 6–8 weeks. The written scope sets the date for each engagement.

Questions

Why does this matter more now?

AI tools make it cheap to try many more strategies and configurations. The more configurations are tried, the more likely it is that the best-looking one owes its rank to luck. Building a backtest has become cheap; knowing whether to believe it has not.

What do you need from us?

Runnable code or scripts, the checks you use with their cutoffs, the data source and licence, and the strength and form of edge you want to detect. By default you provide the data. Where a licence bars passing data to a third party, our scripts can run in your environment, and we receive the output statistics and log hashes but none of the data; the report then says so. You confirm that your licence permits the analysis; a review does not start on data whose licence bars it.

What is it not?

Investment advice, a forecast of performance, an endorsement of past results, a trading recommendation, or anything connected with issuing or promoting a token. Performance claims, strategy tuning and regulatory opinions are outside every review.

Who sees the report, and how can it be cited?

A report is for the client named on it, and the client decides who receives it. Nothing about a client, or about a manager under review, is published without that party's consent. Cite findings with the words "findings, no assurance", as in "VALID checklist score N of 12 (findings, no assurance)". A review is not an approval of a strategy.

Does the SEC Marketing Rule require this?

The rule treats backtested performance as hypothetical performance. An adviser it covers may not advertise it unless, among other conditions, the adviser gives the audience enough information to understand the criteria used and the assumptions made. The rule does not require a third-party review, and a review is not a legal or compliance opinion.

The code is public. Why pay?

The code is public so that you can check us. What a client pays for is a measurement by a third party, designed around your model, labels, grid and cost rules, with pass rules fixed before it runs. Security tools are open source, and companies still hire outside testers to run them on their own systems.

What if an error is found later?

The reviewer corrects or withdraws the report and tells the client in writing. If your stated results cannot be reproduced from your code and data, the review stops and says so.

Correction, October 2026

Preparing a journal version, we re-derived every number in the accompanying paper, checked every method description against its code, and tried to locate every paper in its literature audit. Most of the paper's empirical results did not survive, and we have withdrawn them: the literature audit, the CPCV/PBO false-positive result, the multiple-testing survivors and the parameter-space variance comparison. The audit sheet had been produced by AI-assisted search and never checked against the papers. Several statistics had been computed under a different definition from the one printed.

What still stands, after a re-count of the corpus (E16): a majority of our strategy variants lose money after 18 bp costs (165 of 278, each result counted once), and AUC screening alone flags 27.0% (BTC), 30.0% (ETH) and 26.5% (SOL) of signal-free series as positive. The twelve checklist items are unchanged as a proposal; what is withdrawn is the evidence the paper claimed for them.

A pre-registered re-measurement (E14) then found that PBO, computed with the paper's rank statistic on refitted CPCV splits, almost never passes at its 0.20 threshold, with or without a planted signal. A pre-registered robustness study (E15) found that canonical CSCV does move with a planted signal, but also passes 13.0–15.5% of demeaned series without one at 0.20, and 23% when the drift is kept. For item V4 we now recommend reporting PBO as a description of the search, with its construction named, rather than using it as a gate. The permutation test passes more planted-signal series than signal-free ones but runs at about three times its nominal 5%; permutations that keep the dependence between neighbouring labels run closer to 5%, at a cost in power (E15).

A third pre-registered study (E17) measured canonical-CSCV PBO thresholds over 216 synthetic designs. At 0.20, sets of strategies with equal means pass 3.1–23.7% of the time depending on the design; sets that differ only in how much they lose usually pass more often (211 of 216 designs); and a set with one edge passes about as often as the same set with the edge removed. A low PBO alone does not show an edge.

A pre-registered follow-up (E18), designed after E17's results were known, added the probability of loss beside PBO. In the reference cell the two together told a set with one edge from the same set with the edge removed (29.2% against 2.6% of series), with weak power, since the set with the edge passed in only 29.2% of series. Across the 144 i.i.d. and GARCH designs the probability of loss did not hold a fixed level. Under MA10 dependence in short samples, an embargo between CSCV blocks overshot, and the study makes no recommendation for one.
